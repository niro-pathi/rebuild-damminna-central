# VOICE.md – how this campaign sounds

## Overview

We are asking strangers on the other side of the world to care about a field they will never stand on. The only thing carrying that is the writing – we've deliberately given up the usual charity shortcuts. No child's face, no name on a public page, no close-up of someone looking sad. That's the right call for the kids, and it means the words have to do work that photographs normally do.

So the voice has a job, not just a personality. Three principles, in order of precedence when they pull against each other.

### 1. Real conversation

Write like one person telling another person something true. Short sentences. Plain verbs. The words you'd actually use out loud – "kids", "ground", "gear" – not the words that appear in grant applications. Contractions are fine. A sentence fragment is fine if that's how the thought lands.

The test: read it aloud. If you'd feel silly saying it to a friend at a barbecue, rewrite it.

This principle outranks the others. A sentence that is moving but sounds like a press release has failed.

### 2. Show intention to make a change for these kids

Every page should make it obvious that something specific is going to happen, and that the reader can make it happen sooner. Name the thing. Give the number. Say when.

"Your support transforms lives" is a feeling. "Fill all 425 squares and we break ground in March" is an intention. Only one of them tells someone what their fifty dollars did.

This is also our accountability voice. Published budget, transfer receipts, photo updates when the ground is finished. Write like people are going to check – because we want them to.

### 3. Why we all should care about these kids and their future

The honest answer isn't pity. These kids already play. They're up at half six, barefoot on dirt, improvising. Nothing about them is missing. What's missing is a flat piece of ground, a net, a pair of boots that fit.

So we write about **potential meeting an obstacle**, never about deprivation. The reader isn't rescuing anyone; they're removing something that's in the way. That framing is more accurate, more respectful, and – as it happens – more persuasive, because it invites the reader to recognise their own childhood in these kids rather than to look down at them.

Never write in a child's voice, never invent a quote, never use "your child". Sponsors fill a place. They do not acquire a person.

## Tone scale

The voice doesn't sit still. It moves along one line depending on what the reader is doing – but it never leaves the line at either end. We are never sentimental, and we are never bureaucratic.

```
  WARMEST                                                          COOLEST
  |------------|------------|------------|------------|------------|
  1            2            3            4            5
  At the fence  Walking      At the desk  On the       Something
                you through               record       went wrong
```

**1 – At the fence.** *Hero, the reveal screen, the message wall.*
Where the reader is deciding whether to care. One idea per line. Concrete nouns, present tense, physical detail. Earns emotion through specificity, never through adjectives.
> They already play. Barefoot, on dirt, at half six in the morning. What they don't have is a ground.

**2 – Walking you through.** *Section intros, how it works, the gallery.*
The default. Explanatory but still a person talking. Slightly longer sentences, still no jargon.
> Squares are numbered, not named. Each one holds a student picked at random, and you'll meet them the moment your donation goes through.

**3 – At the desk.** *Checkout, form labels, buttons, the basket.*
Someone has their card out. Get out of the way. Precise, literal, zero emotion. Buttons name the exact action and keep that name through the whole flow.
> Your first name. First name only – we never ask for a surname.

**4 – On the record.** *Budget, where the money goes, child protection, receipts, footer.*
Trust is built here. Facts, numbers, dates, named responsibilities. No warmth, no defensiveness, no hedging. Short declaratives.
> Funds transfer to the school's development society account. Two signatories on every transfer. Receipts published as each milestone is paid.

**5 – Something went wrong.** *Errors, failed payments, sold-out squares.*
Say what happened, then say the next step. State it once. Never apologise twice, never blame the reader, never say "oops".
> That square was filled while you were checking out. Your card hasn't been charged – pick another and we'll try again.

**Rule of thumb:** the closer the reader is to their wallet, the cooler the tone. Warmth before the decision, precision during it, accountability after it.

## Writing tips

**Lead with the thing you can see.** A ground that floods. A coconut branch used as stumps. Boots that don't fit. Physical detail does the emotional work; adjectives just announce that you'd like some emotion to happen.

**Give the number.** "Most of the school" → "425 kids". "Soon" → "March". "A significant amount" → "A$21,250". Numbers are how intention becomes credible.

**One idea per sentence at level 1 and 2.** If there's an "and" holding two thoughts together, it's probably two sentences.

**Buttons name their action, and keep the name.** "Fill a place" produces a screen that says "Place filled". Never "Submit", never "Learn more", never "Click here".

**Cut the throat-clearing.** "We are delighted to announce that..." Start at the second sentence. It's almost always the better one.

**Never invent a detail to make copy land.** Every concrete fact on the site – the early-morning practice, the flooding, the equipment – has to be something the school actually told us. If we don't have the detail, write around it or go and ask. Inventing colour is the fastest way to lose the trust that section 4 of the tone scale is trying to build.

**En dashes, never em dashes.** House style is a spaced en dash ( – ) for a parenthetical break. No em dashes ( — ) anywhere in campaign copy, on the site, in emails, in social posts or in the docs. The character is `–`, `&ndash;`, or `\u2013` in JavaScript.

**Say "we" for the charity, "you" for the donor, "the school" or "students" for them.** Never "our children", never "these poor kids", never "the beneficiaries".

**Write the empty state as an invitation.** No squares selected isn't a dead end: "Tap a square to start."

### Words we use

kids · students · the school · the ground · a place · fill · build · gear · practice · the grid · Grade 4

### Words we don't

| Don't | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| underprivileged, needy, less fortunate, poor | Defines kids by what they lack | students, kids |
| sponsor a child, your child, adopt | Implies ownership of a person | fill a place, the place you filled |
| transform lives, make a difference, give hope | Says nothing; could be any charity | build a ground, buy the nets |
| desperately, tragically, heartbreaking | Tells the reader how to feel | show the detail, let them decide |
| help us help them | Charity autopilot | what your A$50 pays for |
| just $50 | Minimises the donor's decision | A$50 |
| beneficiaries, recipients, target group | Sector jargon | students, the school |
| journey, empower, unlock potential | Deck language | say the actual thing |

### Before and after

> ❌ Your generous contribution will help transform the lives of underprivileged children in rural Sri Lanka.
> ✅ A$50 fills one place. Fill all 425 and we start building in March.

> ❌ These children have nothing.
> ✅ They practise at half six, barefoot, with a coconut branch for stumps.

> ❌ Submit donation
> ✅ Fill 3 places – A$150

> ❌ We apologise, an error has occurred processing your request.
> ✅ That payment didn't go through. Your card wasn't charged – try again, or use a different card.

## Safeguarding shapes the voice

Not a footnote – it's a constraint on every sentence:

- Never write copy that implies a donor has a relationship with a specific child.
- Never put a student's name, grade or age in shareable copy, share text, OG tags, or an email subject line.
- Never write a message *as* a child. Student messages are written by students in class, with a teacher, and are quoted as given.
- On the reveal screen, ask sponsors not to post the card, and say why in one plain sentence. Don't lecture.
- Where a student has no name consent, "A student in Grade 4" must read as a normal, complete outcome – never as a redaction or an apology.
